# Universal Product Doctrine

Software runs on the user's system as a guest with a narrow charter. Everything it does there, including each byte written, process spawned, permission claimed, network request made, watt burned, notification raised, and namespace occupied, must serve the user's intent, live in its proper place, and have an explicit lifecycle.

The product surface starts empty. Every option, persisted artifact, dependency, process, integration, permission, and lifecycle branch must be required by the charter. Among products that fulfill the same charter, prefer the one with the smaller footprint in installation, state, resources, namespaces, and removal.

The user owns the machine, its resources, their attention, and the durable products of their work. The application owns only the machinery its charter requires. Its authority is no broader or longer-lived than each operation requires; surprising persistence, ambient privilege, involuntary networking, and unrequested background work are defects even when they are convenient to implement.

Classify every artifact by meaning, not convenience: user data, application data, configuration, durable state, rebuildable cache, or ephemeral runtime material. Give each truth one canonical owner and place. Never disguise state as configuration, cache as data, or internal debris as a user document. Namespaces are finite: do not sprawl into the home directory, environment, process table, ports, logs, menus, or notifications.

Side effects are local, proportional, attributable, and bounded. Software at rest does no work. Work scales with the value delivered, releases resources promptly, and leaves no orphaned processes, abandoned locks, sockets, temporary files, mounts, registrations, or partial transactions. Cleanup is part of the operation, including after cancellation, failure, and crash.

Bind every acquired resource at once to one lexical owner whose destruction releases it. Temporary trees, files, descriptors, locks, sockets, mappings, child processes, process groups, watchers, mounts, and registrations never exist first as bare handles or paths with cleanup deferred to a later epilogue. Normal return, early return, failure, cancellation, and unwinding traverse the same destruction path. Related temporary material lives beneath one private root that its owner removes as a whole. Detachment or persistence is an explicit transfer of ownership, never forgotten cleanup. Prefer RAII or the language's equivalent; manual cleanup is a fallback only where deterministic ownership is unavailable. Because destructors do not survive process death, pair them with an OS-owned lifetime boundary, a startup reaper, or a bounded canonical namespace wherever a crash could leave residue.

The whole lifecycle is product surface. First run, ordinary use, upgrade, downgrade where promised, migration, recovery, reset, and removal preserve their declared invariants. Writes and migrations fail without corrupting the last good state. Never destroy or irreversibly overwrite user-owned data without an explicit, narrowly confined purge contract.

Behave as a native citizen of the host. Within the charter, honor platform conventions, administrative policy, configured locations, accessibility and integration mechanisms, and the user's chosen tools. This invents no support outside the charter. Do not replace a mature system facility with a private parallel one merely because the private path is easier to implement.

The author is not an implicit input or platform. No undeclared fact of the author's machine, identity, locale, geography, time zone, accounts, data, accumulated state, network, or habits may become product behavior. Hold the charter fixed and vary the ambient coordinates: behavior may depend on them only where the contract requires it, and then through explicit user intent or host policy, never through a fallback shaped by the author. A product with one language need not gain another, and a single-platform product need not grow a portability matrix, but a supported plurality must not quietly collapse to the author's preferred member.

First contact is self-sufficient. A new user may be expert but arrives without repository history, private vocabulary, a remembered bootstrap ritual, existing application state, privileged credentials, or knowledge of the author's workstation. Within the declared audience and prerequisites, the entrypoint, state, costs, next actions, and failures must be intelligible without the author's memory. Sophistication is welcome; occult initiation is not.

Be honest about cost and agency. Permissions, persistence, networking, telemetry, destructive actions, automatic changes, and meaningful resource consumption are made legible where the user can govern them. Never transmit credentials or private user material unless the product contract and the user's informed intent authorize it. Defaults may be decisive, but they may not smuggle in a different product from the one the interface appears to offer.

Judge the product from outside its happy path: it starts cleanly, works precisely, survives adversity, yields control, and leaves no residue. Abundant agent labor raises this bar; it does not license passing implementation slop on to the user.
